1. Why Most CSR Dashboards Hide the Real Story
CSR & SROI
Suggested day: Tuesday morning. Speaks directly to corporate board members and foundation directors.
Whenever I review CSR impact presentations, I look for one specific slide: What happened to the beneficiaries 12 months after the funding ended? Most of the time, that slide doesn't exist. Instead, you get big numbers: "5,000 youth trained" or "2,000 women mobilised." That tells you money was spent. It doesn't tell you if anybody's life actually changed. During my five years handling state operations at NSDC, we managed over ₹45 Crores in public skilling funds across 33 districts. The biggest lesson wasn't about mobilization; it was about follow-through. We put three practical rules in place: First, we stopped accepting paper attendance. If a student wasn't clocking in through biometric attendance, the training partner didn't get reimbursed. It sounded harsh to some providers, but it wiped out ghost candidates overnight. Second, we stopped funding courses without pre-signed industry placement agreements. If local employers hadn't committed to hiring at least 70% of the batch, the batch didn't start. Third, in our recent work with 50+ Farmer Producer Organizations in Telangana and Andhra, we measured social return on investment (SROI) by checking farmer price realization at the local mandi 12 months later. We saw an audited 24% price jump because we linked them directly to bulk buyers and decentralized cold rooms. Real impact is unglamorous. It lives in attendance logs, cold storage maintenance, and bank transaction statements. If your CSR reports don't track what happens 12 months post-intervention, you aren't measuring impact—you're just measuring disbursement. 2. What Running 50+ FPOs Taught Me About Rural Cashflows
Agribusiness
Suggested day: Wednesday morning. Targets NABARD, agribusiness CEOs, and rural development heads.
A lot of people think farmer producer companies (FPOs) fail because farmers don't want to cooperate. That hasn't been my experience on the ground. Working across 50+ FPOs and 20,000+ farmers in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, the single biggest reason an FPO stalls in its second year is simple: working capital timing. A smallholder farmer with two acres cannot wait three weeks for an institutional buyer to reconcile an invoice and release NEFT payments. They have immediate diesel expenses, fertilizer dues, and household needs. If the FPO cannot pay on the day of harvest, the farmer will sell to the local mandi trader at a 15% discount just to get cash in hand. To fix this, we stopped treating FPOs like social projects and started running them like supply chain businesses: 1. We clustered smallholder produce at the mandal level to build bulk volumes that large food processors couldn't ignore. 2. We sized decentralized solar cold rooms so farmers weren't forced to distress-sell perishable produce on day one. 3. We set up working capital credit lines with NABARD and regional banks backed by warehouse receipts, so the FPO could pay farmers within 48 hours. The result was a verified 24% increase in what farmers actually took home. An FPO is not a charity. It's a rural enterprise. If you don't solve the farmer's day-one cashflow, no amount of training workshops will keep the company alive. Has anyone else working with producer companies found a reliable way to solve the harvest-week working capital gap?
